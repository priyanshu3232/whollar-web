How much internet speed do you actually need?

Almost certainly less than you are paying for. Here are the real numbers, activity by activity.

Whollar·~6 min read

Internet speed is the rare product people buy in a size they have never measured, for activities they have never priced in megabits.

So plans are sold on a simple instinct: bigger is safer. And bigger is genuinely safer, in the way a ten-bedroom house is safer for a family of three. The question this post answers with real numbers is the one the plan pages never quite do: how much internet speed do you actually need for the things your household actually does?

The short version
  • 50 to 100 Mbps comfortably runs a typical household, including simultaneous 4K streams, video calls, and browsing. The CRTC (Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission) sets its universal target for every Canadian home at 50 down, 10 up.
  • A single 4K stream needs about 15 Mbps. A full HD stream about 5. A video call, 3 to 4. Online gaming, 3 to 6, though what gaming really needs is low latency, not bandwidth.
  • 1 gig is more than sixty 4K streams of capacity. Superb for big households and huge downloads, unused headroom for most homes.
  • The tier you never use is money on the table every month, and the gap between tiers is often $20 to $40.
  • Right-size first, then make providers compete for the right-sized plan. That order saves twice.

How much internet speed do I need? The honest numbers

Most households need 50 to 100 Mbps, and the arithmetic behind that is short. Internet demand is just the sum of what runs at the same moment. Netflix's own guidance puts a 4K stream at 15 Mbps and a full HD stream at 5. A group video call uses 3 to 4. Music, browsing, and messaging barely register. Stack a realistic busy evening, one 4K show, one HD show on a tablet, a video call, and background devices, and you are near 30 Mbps of actual concurrent demand. A 100 Mbps plan carries that with two thirds of its capacity idle, which is exactly the comfortable margin you want, and it is why the CRTC set 50/10 as the universal service objective for every Canadian home rather than something gaudier.

Is 1 gig internet good?

A gigabit connection is excellent, and that is not the interesting question. One gig is about 1,000 Mbps: more than sixty simultaneous 4K streams of capacity, or a full 80 GB game landing in roughly eleven minutes instead of two hours. If your household moves huge files routinely, runs many heavy users at once, or simply values never thinking about the pipe, gig service delivers exactly what it promises. The interesting question is what it delivers to a household streaming two shows and answering email, and the honest answer is: the same evening they would have had at 100 Mbps, at a higher price. Speed you never touch does not make pages load faster or calls clearer. It is headroom, and headroom is only worth what you would pay knowing it is headroom.

Nobody regrets the speed they have. The bill is where the regret hides.

The two numbers that matter more than the headline

Two things shape your daily experience more than the download tier. Upload speed is the half of the plan the banner rarely mentions, and it is what video calls, cloud backups, and sending large files actually lean on; a plan with healthy upload at a modest tier often feels better than a huge download tier with a thin return path. And latency, the delay before data moves at all, is what gaming and calls live or die on. Latency comes from the quality and technology of the connection, not the size of the tier, which is why upgrading from 500 Mbps to a gig does nothing for a laggy game. If your connection feels bad at a tier the table above says should be plenty, the fix is usually the line or the router, not a bigger plan.

Why households end up oversized

Not by accident. Speed is the one lever a provider can sell upward forever, and fear of slowness is free to manufacture, so plan pages anchor on the biggest number and let "just to be safe" do the rest. The pattern pairs neatly with the promo cliff: the oversized tier arrives discounted, the discount expires, and the household is left paying full freight for capacity it never once saturated. Meanwhile the gap between tiers runs $20 to $40 a month, which compounds into the same quiet overpayment this whole series is about. Right-sizing is the rare fix that costs nothing and works immediately.

How to right-size in ten minutes

Count your genuinely simultaneous heavy uses on a worst evening, price them from the table, add half again as margin, and you have your number. Then check what that tier costs across providers on your line, because the same cable is often sold at two very different prices for the identical speed. Most households land at 100 Mbps and discover they have been paying for three to ten times that. The savings from stepping down a tier, and the savings from making providers compete for the right-sized plan, stack. That second half is what Whollar's cohorts are being built to do: pool households by region and renewal window so providers bid for the group, right-sized plans included. Frequently asked questions

How much internet speed do I need?

For most households, 50 to 100 Mbps covers everything comfortably, including simultaneous 4K streaming, video calls, and browsing. The CRTC's own universal service target for every Canadian home is 50 Mbps down and 10 up. Go higher when many heavy users share the connection or you move very large files often.

Is 1 gig internet good?

A gigabit connection is excellent and more than almost any household can use: it is more than sixty simultaneous 4K streams of capacity. It is good value only if you regularly move huge files or have many heavy users at once. For typical homes, the same money buys the same experience at a lower tier.

How many Mbps do I need for gaming?

Bandwidth is the wrong number to watch: online play itself uses only about 3 to 6 Mbps. What gaming actually needs is low, stable latency, which depends on the connection quality, not the tier. The place speed matters is game downloads, where a bigger pipe shortens the wait.

What is a decent internet speed for working from home?

A stable 25 to 50 Mbps handles video meetings, cloud documents, and screen sharing with room to spare, since an HD group call needs only a few Mbps each way. Upload speed and stability matter more than the headline download number for calls, so check both when comparing plans.
